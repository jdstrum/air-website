Technology infrastructure

Give each workload
the right home.

Build or buy. Open or proprietary models. GPUs on demand or dedicated capacity. The right choices depend on the work, and on how your data, network, and people will support it.

What this means for your business ↓

The network your people rely on

A stronger network.
Less on your team.

Your AI plans depend on the same foundation as the rest of your business: reliable Wi-Fi, wired connections, and internet access across the places people work.

Who keeps it running, and how much of that responsibility belongs on your team?

Choose how it gets managed.

Run the network internally, share responsibility with a partner, or evaluate network as a service. Compare what is included: design, installation, hardware, monitoring, support, and upgrades. Make ownership of outages and recovery clear.

Compare the full commitment.

Weigh equipment purchases and refresh cycles against recurring service costs. Include internal staff time, licenses, internet service, contract terms, and what happens when you add a location or leave the service.

Use the next change as an opening.

An office move, expansion, aging equipment, recurring Wi-Fi problems, or an overstretched IT team can all prompt a better network decision. You do not need a GPU project to start this conversation.

The decisions behind the deployment

Choose the capability.
Then the commitment.

These choices are connected, but each needs its own evaluation. Building an application does not mean training a model or buying GPUs.

Build or buy?+

Compare configuring what you already own, connecting existing systems, buying a suitable product, and commissioning custom work. A custom application can still use a managed model service.

What will the provider deliver, what must your team contribute, and who supports the result after launch?

Open source, open weights, or proprietary?+

Compare model quality on your tasks, deployment options, support, and the rights to use or modify it. Open weights make model parameters available; they do not automatically mean unrestricted use or a fully open-source system.

A proprietary service may simplify operations. An open model may offer more deployment flexibility. Neither label alone tells you how your data is protected.

How much control do you need, and what can your team responsibly operate?

Use a service, rent GPUs, or own capacity?+

A managed AI service handles the underlying compute. GPU-as-a-service lets you rent accelerator capacity. Dedicated or owned infrastructure puts more of the environment, and its operating responsibility, in your hands.

Running a model to produce answers is inference. Fine-tuning and training place different demands on memory, storage, and compute. Size for the actual model, workload, and concurrent demand.

Compare utilization, response time, staffing, and total operating cost. For owned hardware, include power, cooling, and refresh cycles.

Cloud, on-premises, colocation, or a blend?+

Put each workload where its data requirements, response time, capacity, and economics make sense. You may keep sensitive data close while using remote compute, or run a time-sensitive task near the people and systems it serves.

What needs to stay, what can move, and what has to remain connected?

Can we get more from what we already spend?+

Before adding capacity, look at what is underused, oversized, or no longer needed. Compare cloud placement, service commitments, and the support required to migrate and run the environment.

For applications billed by AI usage, review which teams and tasks drive the cost. Evaluate model selection and repeated requests against the quality, privacy, and response times the work needs.

Where can you reduce waste while protecting the performance your business relies on?

Connect the whole workload

The model runs somewhere.
Your data has to reach it.

A GPU can be available while the data path is still the bottleneck. Map where information lives, where processing happens, and where the result needs to return.

Connect across environments.

Private interconnection can link supported clouds, data centers, storage, and compute providers. Direct cloud-to-cloud routing can avoid an unnecessary round trip through your own data center.

Change capacity with the work.

Software-defined connections can make bandwidth easier to adjust for migrations, data transfers, and changing demand. Availability, existing access, and provider terms determine what can be provisioned and when.

Count the cost of moving data.

Review transfer volume, cloud egress, routing charges, ports, and connections alongside compute. A different path may improve the economics; compare the complete design rather than assume savings.

Keep the whole path accountable.

Private interconnection complements branch access and SD-WAN; it does not replace every connection. Plan redundant paths and recovery, and preserve identity, encryption, and security controls.

Private connectivity is worth evaluating when data spans environments or performance, control, or transfer costs become a constraint. The right answer starts with your traffic and requirements.
