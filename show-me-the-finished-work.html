Field perspective · September 2026

Show me
the finished work.

A platform can promise a great deal. Buyers still need to see what their team will actually receive.

An AIR perspective informed by recent conversations with technology sales and delivery specialists. These are observations from a limited set of opportunities, not a market-wide survey.

A theme surfaced in recent conversations about AI opportunities: explaining what a platform could build did not always answer the buyer’s question. Some buyers wanted to see the finished report, application, or workflow they would use. They needed to judge whether it would improve on what they already had.

That distinction matters when a small team is expected to turn a flexible platform into a working business tool. The capability may be there. The people, time, and delivery support needed to make it useful are a separate part of the purchase.

Start with a recognizable piece of work.

A broad demonstration can help explain possibilities. For a buying decision, ask the provider to follow one task your team recognizes, from the information coming in to the result someone needs.

For example, an illustrative reporting workflow might bring together approved records, explain a variance, and produce a report a manager can check. Seeing how to configure the system is useful. Seeing the report, its sources, and how a person corrects an error answers a different question.

Agree what the demonstration is intended to prove. A concept, a configured prototype, and a production-ready application represent different stages. Have the provider identify what works today, what remains to be built, and which assumptions still need testing.

Look beyond the smoothest path.

Ask what happens when information is missing, a source is unavailable, or the answer needs review. Watch the time it takes to return a useful result. Explore how someone finds the evidence, corrects the output, or reaches help.

A polished screenshot can explain an idea. A working demonstration lets you inspect behavior. Neither establishes performance across all of your workloads; a focused pilot may still be needed before a larger commitment.

Include the people who will deliver it.

Ask who will configure the application, connect the systems, test it, and support it after launch. What must your team supply? How much specialist time does the proposal assume you have?

The answer might be a product your team can manage, a provider who implements it, or a combination. Compare the complete delivery arrangement. A tool that makes development easier can still require skills and capacity your business needs to source.

Make the next conversation advance the decision.

Before another meeting, ask for an agreed output: a working example, a scoped proposal, or answers to the requirements still unresolved. Clarify any pilot charges and what will be delivered for them. Repeating a general presentation rarely settles those details.

At AIR, we compare provider options, bring solutions architects and technical specialists into the conversation, and follow up on supplier commitments. The provider is responsible for delivering the detailed work it proposes.

Take this into your next conversation

“Show us the result our team would use, explain what it takes to deliver it, and identify what we still need to validate.”
