The human test

Faster
for whom?

Before you automate a customer or employee interaction, follow the work all the way through.

Explore the experience layer ↗

Put this beside the demo.

Imagine an assistant closes a request quickly, but the customer contacts you again because the problem remains. The first interaction looks efficient. The whole experience tells a different story.

Ask for the full journey.

  • What happens when the request is ambiguous, sensitive, or outside the system’s authority?
  • Can someone reach a person, and does the context travel with them?
  • Who checks whether the issue was resolved, and whether work simply moved to the customer or another team?

A reason to pause.

The proposal measures success only in shorter interactions or fewer human contacts. Ask for resolution, repeat contacts, effort, and service quality as well.

Try this next

Choose one recurring request. Trace it with the people handling it, agree what “better” means, and test a small change with a workable human fallback.

The cost test

After
the pilot.

A starting price tells you very little about the commitment your team will operate.

Explore the infrastructure ↗

Ask for a working cost picture.

Use your expected workload and a higher-demand scenario. Ask the supplier to identify which costs are included, variable, capped, or still unknown. Keep your team’s operating time in the comparison.

  • Usage: users, requests, model calls, storage, and peak demand.
  • Connections: integration, data transfer, networking, and access controls.
  • Operations: support, monitoring, review, maintenance, and training.
  • Change: minimum commitments, renewal terms, exporting your data, and moving away.

A reason to pause.

The proposal relies on pilot volumes, assumes someone else will maintain the integration, or cannot explain how you leave. Resolve those assumptions before treating the price as a complete comparison.

Try this next

Ask each shortlisted supplier to price the same workload and document exclusions. Ask for the basis of the price, the work included, and the assumptions that could change it. Include implementation, support, human review, and existing contract commitments when comparing options.

The need test

Another tool?
Maybe not.

Make the problem specific before adding another subscription to the portfolio.

Explore strategic direction ↗

Look at the work you already fund.

Pick one task that takes too long or fails too often. Identify where it stalls: missing information, unclear ownership, duplicated entry, a broken connection, or a capability you do not have.

  • Could an existing platform support the task with configuration, training, or a better connection?
  • Would a clearer process solve the problem without new software?
  • If something new is needed, who will own its use, support, and results?

A reason to pause.

The proposed solution has no clear owner, no defined workflow, or no way to tell whether it helps. Buying it will not settle those questions.

Try this next

Write a one-page brief: the task, the bottleneck, what you already own, and the improvement you need. Use it to compare a process change, an existing capability, and a new purchase.

Ask for a working demonstration of the task you need completed. Look at the finished output, the time it takes, and what happens when information is missing. Have the provider identify what is working now and what still needs to be built.
